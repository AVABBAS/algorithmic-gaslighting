Response
In high-pressure environments, even the most fundamental cognitive tasks—such as simple
arithmetic—are susceptible to failure. This phenomenon is extensively studied in the fields of
cognitive psychology and human factors engineering.
Below is a structured overview of why these errors occur, which you may find useful for your report:
1. Working Memory Depletion
Working memory has a finite capacity. In high-pressure situations, a significant portion of this
capacity is redirected toward "secondary tasks," such as:
     • Anxiety Management: Processing the fear of failure or the consequences of an error.
     • Monitoring: Over-analyzing one’s own performance rather than the task at hand.
This leaves fewer cognitive resources available for actual computation, leading to "choking" or
simple calculation slips.
2. The Speed-Accuracy Trade-off
Under time constraints, the brain often prioritizes speed over precision. This leads to:
     • Heuristic Processing: Relying on mental shortcuts or "gut feelings" rather than systematic
       calculation.
     • Premature Closure: Finishing a calculation as soon as a plausible-looking result is reached,
       without performing a verification step.
3. Cognitive Tunneling (Fixation)
Under stress, the human visual and mental focus narrows. While this can help with concentration, it
often leads to:
     • Ignoring Context: Missing obvious red flags or "sanity checks" (e.g., a result that is
       mathematically impossible within the given context).
     • Repetitive Error: Repeating the same incorrect calculation because the brain is "locked" into
       a specific logical path.
4. Physiological Effects of Fatigue
Since you mentioned feeling "fried," fatigue is a critical factor. Exhaustion leads to:
     • Reduced Synaptic Efficiency: Slower communication between neurons in the prefrontal
       cortex, which governs logical reasoning.
     • Lapses in Attention: Brief "microsleeps" or mental absences where a digit or a decimal point
       is simply overlooked.
5. Confirmation Bias
In high-stakes reporting, there is often an unconscious desire for the data to lead to a certain
conclusion. This can cause a professional to:
     • Inadvertently overlook an arithmetic error that supports their hypothesis.
     • Double-check only the figures that seem "wrong" while ignoring errors in figures that look
       "correct."
Mitigation Strategies for Your Report:
To address these errors in a professional setting, organizations typically implement:
     • External Verification: Having a second person or software validate the math.
     • Checklists: Forcing a step-by-step verification process.
     • Rest Cycles: Acknowledging that cognitive precision drops significantly after long periods of
       continuous work.
If you need a more detailed analysis of any of these specific points for your report, please let me
know.

        payment), the breaching party is ordered to return that benefit. The goal is to prevent the
        breaching party from being "unjustly enriched."
4. Reformation
In cases where the contract was poorly drafted or contains a clerical error that does not reflect the
parties’ actual intent, the court may "reform" or rewrite the contract to reflect the true agreement.
This is less common in material breach cases and more common in disputes over interpretation.
5. Mitigation of Damages (A Key Requirement)
It is important to note that the non-breaching party has a legal "duty to mitigate." This means you
must take reasonable steps to minimize the financial losses resulting from the breach. You cannot
recover damages for losses that could have been avoided through reasonable effort (e.g., failing to
look for a new supplier when the original one fails to deliver).
